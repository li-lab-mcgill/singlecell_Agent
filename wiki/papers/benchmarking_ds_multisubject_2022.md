## Summary
Performs a comprehensive benchmark of 18 methods for cell-type-specific differential state (DS) detection in multisubject scRNA-seq, finding that per-sample pseudobulk aggregation (especially sum aggregation) and single-cell mixed models that model subjects as random effects control false positives best, while naïve cell-level methods have inflated false positive rates. The results support using donors/samples as the replication unit for inference in multisubject scRNA-seq.

## Hypothesis framed
In multisubject scRNA-seq DS analysis, methods that account for subject-level variation (pseudobulk aggregation or mixed models with subject as random effect) will control false positives and FDR better than naïve cell-level methods that treat cells as independent.

## Questions answered
- Do pseudobulk aggregation methods control FDR/precision closer to the nominal 0.05 than naïve single-cell methods for multisubject scRNA-seq DS analysis?
- Do single-cell mixed models that include subject as a random effect outperform naïve cell-level methods in controlling false positives?
- Does latent-variable modeling (as implemented in Seurat-style approaches) rescue naïve single-cell methods from pseudoreplication bias?

## Key findings
Pseudobulk methods achieved the highest overall AUROC and were the only class of methods to attain precision/FDR levels close to the nominal 0.05, yielding the best specificity and precision. Mixed models that include subjects as random effects performed comparably to pseudobulk methods in controlling false positives. Naïve single-cell methods and latent-variable models showed higher sensitivity but produced many false positives (low precision). Sum (raw-count) pseudobulk aggregation followed by bulk normalization outperformed mean aggregation. Method rankings were stable across different numbers of cells and samples.

## Methods used
Compared 18 methods grouped into pseudobulk (sum/mean aggregation analyzed with DESeq2, limma, edgeR, ROTS), single-cell mixed models (subject as random effect), naïve single-cell methods (assume cell independence), and latent-variable models. Evaluated via simulations from a reference-free negative binomial generative model (NEBULA), across varying numbers of cells and samples. Performance metrics: AUROC, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1 score, Matthews correlation coefficient; binary summaries used FDR threshold 0.05.

## Method and dataset
Methods were evaluated on simulated multisubject scRNA-seq count data generated by a NEBULA reference-free negative binomial model, with experimental designs varying numbers of cells per sample and numbers of samples per condition. The study compared per-sample pseudobulk aggregation (sum and mean) analyzed with bulk-DE tools and single-cell mixed models assuming subject-level random effects. Assumptions: count data follow a negative binomial generative process and biological replicate (subject) structure is the primary source of between-sample variation.

## Limitations
Benchmarking primarily used simulations from a single negative binomial generative model which may not capture all properties of real datasets; not all method parameterizations, normalization strategies, or real-data scenarios were exhaustively explored; results may depend on simulation design and parameter choices; study focused on scRNA-seq and did not provide specific examples for scATAC-seq.

## Extends or contradicts
Extends prior empirical recommendations favoring pseudobulk aggregation and mixed models over naïve cell-level tests for multisubject single-cell DE/DS analysis; contradicts the practice of using cell-level naïve tests for inferential claims because they produce inflated false positives due to pseudoreplication.

## Boundary conditions
Works when: Applies to multisubject scRNA-seq experiments where biological replicates (subjects/samples) are present and modeled either by per-sample pseudobulk aggregation (preferably sum aggregation with bulk normalization) or by mixed models including subject as a random effect; performance was stable across varying numbers of cells and samples in the simulated scenarios and at FDR target 0.05.
Fails when: Naïve single-cell methods that treat cells as independent fail (produce excess false positives) in the presence of between-subject variation (pseudoreplication); latent-variable approaches tested did not correct pseudoreplication bias; findings may not generalize when real data violate the NEBULA negative binomial assumptions or in data modalities not tested (e.g., scATAC-seq).
