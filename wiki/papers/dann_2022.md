## Summary
This paper introduces Milo, a differential abundance framework that tests changes in cell-state abundance using partially overlapping neighborhoods on a k-nearest neighbor graph rather than predefined discrete clusters. It matters for disease-control and perturbation scRNA-seq studies because it uses biological samples as the counting unit, supports covariate-adjusted negative binomial models, and can detect abundance shifts along continuous or heterogeneous cell-state structure.

## Hypothesis framed
Differential abundance testing over overlapping neighborhoods on a single-cell KNN graph can detect condition-associated cell-state abundance changes that cluster-based tests miss while maintaining false discovery rate control in the presence of batch effects.

## Questions answered
- Does neighborhood-based differential abundance testing on a KNN graph identify abundance perturbations that are obscured by clustering-based tests in scRNA-seq data?
- Does Milo maintain false discovery rate control when batch effects are present?
- How does Milo compare with Louvain-cluster differential abundance testing and cluster-free methods such as Cydar, DAseq, and MELD?

## Key findings
Milo detected abundance changes missed or obscured by cluster-based analyses, especially when perturbations occurred along continuous trajectories or within heterogeneous clusters. In simulations and scRNA-seq benchmarks, it maintained false discovery rate control across batch effects and generally outperformed Louvain-cluster testing, Cydar, DAseq, and MELD. In applications, it identified an age-associated decline of a fate-biased epithelial precursor in the mouse thymus and perturbations across multiple lineages in human cirrhotic liver.

## Methods used
The authors constructed a k-nearest neighbor graph from single-cell data, selected representative partially overlapping neighborhoods using a refined sampling strategy, counted cells from each biological sample within each neighborhood, and fit negative binomial generalized linear models for differential abundance testing with false discovery rate control across neighborhoods. They evaluated Milo using simulations and scRNA-seq datasets and benchmarked it against Louvain cluster-based testing, Cydar, DAseq, and MELD.

## Method and dataset
Method: Milo neighborhood differential abundance testing using KNN graph neighborhoods and negative binomial GLMs on per-sample neighborhood counts. Data type: scRNA-seq and simulated single-cell data. Biological examples: aging mouse thymus scRNA-seq and human cirrhotic liver scRNA-seq. Approximate dataset size is not reported in the summary. Experimental design: comparisons of abundance between experimental conditions with biological samples as replicates and optional covariates in the design matrix. Assumptions: the KNN graph represents biologically meaningful cell-cell similarity, biological samples are the statistical units for abundance testing, and neighborhood counts can be modeled with a negative binomial GLM.

## Limitations
Milo depends on the quality of the KNN graph and therefore on preprocessing choices including dimensionality reduction, feature selection, batch correction, and the choice of k. Its resolution depends on neighborhood construction parameters, and significant neighborhoods require downstream biological annotation. It detects compositional abundance differences but does not establish causal mechanisms or directly perform molecular differential expression or differential state testing conditional on abundance. Very rare populations, poorly sampled graph regions, and datasets with inadequate biological replication may be difficult to analyze robustly.

## Evidence pattern
Entity definition: defines Milo neighborhoods as partially overlapping regions of a KNN graph. Statistical unit: biological sample-level counts within neighborhoods, not individual cells. Metric/model: negative binomial generalized linear model for neighborhood abundance with FDR correction across neighborhoods. Covariates: supports GLM design matrices for condition and covariate adjustment. Validation: simulations, benchmark scRNA-seq datasets, aging mouse thymus, and human cirrhotic liver applications. Boundary conditions: evaluates batch effects and continuous trajectories where cluster-based approaches can fail.

## Extends or contradicts
This paper extends cluster-based single-cell differential abundance workflows by replacing discrete cluster counts with overlapping KNN-graph neighborhood counts. It does not directly build on or contradict any listed existing wiki paper.

## Boundary conditions
Works when: Works when the dataset has biological sample-level replication, a KNN graph that captures meaningful cell-state similarity, and an experimental design expressible with a negative binomial GLM design matrix. It is especially suited to scRNA-seq studies where abundance changes occur within heterogeneous clusters or along continuous trajectories and where batch or donor covariates need to be included as fixed effects.
Fails when: May fail or become unreliable when preprocessing produces a poor KNN graph, when the choice of k or neighborhood parameters obscures the relevant state resolution, when cell populations are extremely rare or poorly sampled, or when there is inadequate biological replication for sample-level abundance modeling. It does not by itself separate gene-level disease effects from compositional shifts and does not provide a full framework for complex repeated-measures or random-effect donor designs beyond GLM-style covariates.
